# Chapter 31 — Home

Same suitcase. Same taxi. Same house.

The driver does not remember him. Why would he? It has been a month. The city looks the same, which is somehow worse. Robert pays, tips too much, drags the suitcase up the steps to his front door. The key sticks. The hallway smells of closed windows.

He unpacks badly, over three days. The blazers go back in the closet. The book he did not read goes on the shelf. The shells he bought at the first port go in a bowl by the door, where they will stay until he throws them out in the spring.

On the third day, he takes the Rolex out of its case and sets it on the nightstand.

He does not put it on.

He took it off on the last formal night, and he has not put it back on since. In thirty years, he has not gone this long without it. He looks at it there, a small, expensive circle on the dark wood, and he feels something he cannot name. Not loss, exactly. Something closer to relief, and something closer to fear.

Then he opens his laptop and looks at the next October crossing.

The endings diverge here.

---

## OBLIVIOUS

He thinks the trip was a triumph.

He tells the story at dinner with his old colleagues. The masquerade. The woman in the swan dress. The night in the sauna. The group scene afterward, which he describes as "the best night of my life" and which he gets slightly wrong, mixing up the details, adding a person who was not there. They laugh. He laughs. He is a good storyteller. He has always been a good storyteller.

He shows them a photograph. Him on a beach with six people, sunburned and grinning, his arm around a woman whose name he can no longer remember. There are two other people in the photo whose names he never knew. It does not bother him. They were part of the trip. The trip was the point.

He books the next October crossing. Same ship. Same cabin. He tells himself he will do it better next time. He will make real friends. He will stay in touch. He will not just be the funny new one.

The Rolex stays on the nightstand, not on his wrist. He does not notice.

The last frame is the same as the others: the watch on the nightstand, the booking page on the screen. But he is smiling.

---

## WOUNDED

He knows something went wrong, and he knows who to blame.

The crowd. The regulars. The cliques. The table that never really let him in. Hal's words come back to him, *you want a queue*, and he dismisses them again. Hal was always dramatic. Hal was always looking for a reason to be disappointed.

He is angry at Connie, who invited him and then made him feel small. He is angry at Priya, who watched him disappear and did nothing. He is angry at Ellis, who made him feel something real and then left first. He is angry at the whole ship, that beautiful, closed little world, with its inside jokes and its private dinners and its empty seats.

He takes the Rolex off the nightstand. He looks at it. He puts it back.

He does not email Ellis. He does not read the card. He puts it in a drawer with the shells and the boarding pass and the photograph of six people on a beach.

He books a different ship for next October. A bigger one. A better crowd.

The last frame is the same: the watch on the nightstand, the booking page on the screen. But he is frowning.

---

## ALMOST

He knows exactly what he did, and he knows it is fixable.

That is the worst part. It is not a mystery. It is not a tragedy of fate. It is a man at a bar, on a beautiful night, deciding not to cross a room. It is a card in his pocket with an email on it. It is a door left open.

He sits at the kitchen table at eleven at night. The laptop is open. The next October crossing is on the screen, the same route, the same ship, the same thirty days. The house is quiet. The Rolex is on the nightstand in the other room.

He takes out Ellis's card. He looks at the email address. He reads it twice.

Then he opens his email and starts a message.

*Ellis.*

He stops. Deletes it. Types again.

*Ellis. I don't know how to say this without making it a performance. You said I was easy to like and hard to be friends with. You were right. I'm trying to be better at the second thing. I'm not sure I know how. But I'd like to try.*

He reads it twice.

Then he sits there, his hands on the keyboard, the cursor blinking.

**CHOICE: send the email, or don't.**

If he sends it, he closes the laptop and sits in the dark for a while. The house is quiet. The watch is on the nightstand. The booking page is still open on the screen, waiting.

*He'd reply, of course. He'd probably even be pleased. Robert closed the laptop.*

If he doesn't, he saves the draft. He tells himself he will finish it tomorrow. He has been about to write it for thirty years.

*It was late. He'd write it tomorrow. He'd been about to write it for thirty years.*

Either way, the last frame is the same. The Rolex on the nightstand, not on his wrist. The booking page on the screen. Robert at the kitchen table at eleven at night, looking at the next October crossing.
